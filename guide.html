What's Actually on the CAASPP ELA Test

The real CAASPP (Smarter Balanced) ELA test is built around four "claims": Reading, Writing, Listening, and Research/Inquiry. It also includes a longer Performance Task where you read several sources and write a full essay. This practice site focuses on the parts that can be instantly scored — Reading and Language/Editing — since those make up the bulk of your points and are the best ones to drill over and over. You should still practice full essay writing separately (with a teacher or parent reading it), since that's worth a real chunk of your score too.

Big picture: Reading comprehension + solid grammar/editing skills carry most of the ELA score. If you get comfortable finding evidence in a passage and spotting sentence errors quickly, you're most of the way there.

Reading Skills Checklist

Across both Literary and Informational passages, CAASPP questions test whether you can:

  • Find evidence in the text to support an answer (not just "vibes" — point to the actual sentence)
  • Identify the theme or central/main idea and how it develops across the passage
  • Explain how characters, events, or ideas interact and change over the passage
  • Figure out word/phrase meaning from context, including figurative and connotative language
  • Recognize how a passage is structured (chronological, cause/effect, compare/contrast, problem/solution)
  • Identify point of view or purpose — why did the author write this, and from whose perspective?
  • Evaluate an argument — is the evidence actually strong, or is there a gap in the reasoning?
  • Compare or connect two passages on a similar topic

How to Work Through a Reading Passage

  1. Preview first. Glance at the title and skim the first and last paragraph before reading closely — it primes you for the topic and saves time later.
  2. Read actively. For a story, keep track of who wants what and what's stopping them. For an informational text, find the main claim and notice what evidence backs it up.
  3. Answer with evidence. When a question asks "which detail best supports...", go back and re-read that specific part of the passage instead of relying on memory.
  4. On "select two" or "select all that apply" questions, check every single choice — don't stop as soon as one option looks right. There's often a second correct answer hiding later in the list.
  5. Skip and return. If a question is taking too long, flag it and come back after finishing easier ones in the same section — don't let one hard question eat your whole clock.

Editing questions usually ask you to pick the best revision, not just a grammatically possible one — look for the choice that's most concise and keeps a consistent, formal tone.

Grade 7 vs. Grade 8: What Changes

Grade 8 passages tend to run a bit longer and denser, and questions push harder on evaluating an author's reasoning (is the evidence actually convincing?) rather than just locating a fact. You'll also see more questions about tone, style, and how word choice affects meaning. The underlying strategy is identical — just expect to reread a bit more carefully.

Test Day Tips

  • Each practice test gives you about 2.5 minutes per question on average — budget accordingly, but reading passages will naturally eat more of that time up front.
  • There's no penalty for guessing — never leave a question blank.
  • Read all answer choices before picking one, even if the first one looks good.
  • If two answers both seem partly true, pick the one the passage most directly supports.

What's Actually on the CAASPP Math Test

CAASPP Math is built around four claims: Concepts & Procedures (do you know how to do the math), Problem Solving (can you apply it to a real situation), Communicating Reasoning (can you explain why an answer is correct or spot an error in someone else's reasoning), and Data Analysis & Modeling. The practice tests here mix all four styles into every section using multiple-choice, select-all, and fill-in-the-answer question types — just like the real test's mix of selected-response and technology-enhanced items.

Big picture: Read the whole question before you start calculating. A lot of point loss on CAASPP math isn't from not knowing the math — it's from answering the wrong question (e.g. solving for the wrong variable, or forgetting a final step like adding tax).

Problem-Solving Strategy

  1. Read it twice. First pass for the story, second pass to pin down exactly what value you're asked to find.
  2. Estimate first. Get a rough sense of what a reasonable answer looks like before you calculate — it catches careless mistakes fast.
  3. Show your steps on scratch paper, even for "easy" problems — most errors come from skipping steps in your head.
  4. Check by plugging back in. After solving an equation, substitute your answer back into the original problem to confirm it works.
  5. Watch your signs. Negative numbers are the single most common source of small math errors — double-check every subtraction and every negative times negative.
  6. For "select all that apply," test every option against the rule in the question instead of stopping once you find one that fits.

Grade 7 vs. Grade 8: What Changes

Grade 8 leans more heavily into algebra: you'll be expected to solve systems of two equations, work with irrational numbers and exponents/scientific notation, and reason about functions (not just single equations). Geometry also gets more abstract — instead of just computing area/volume, you'll describe and reason about transformations (rotations, reflections, dilations) and congruence/similarity.
